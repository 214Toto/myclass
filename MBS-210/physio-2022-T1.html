Short Essay Questions

1. State two functional differences between plasma membrane calcium ATPase and sodium-calcium exchanger. [2 marks]

Model Answers

1. Energy Source: Calcium ATPase (PMCA) uses primary active transport (directly hydrolyzes ATP), whereas the Sodium-Calcium Exchanger (NCX) uses secondary active transport (uses the electrochemical gradient of sodium).

2. Kinetics/Capacity: Calcium ATPase has a high affinity but low capacity (fine-tuning), while NCX has a low affinity but high capacity (rapidly clearing large calcium loads after an action potential).

2. What do you understand by the vagal escape phenomenon? [2 marks]

Model Answer

Vagal escape is the phenomenon where the ventricles resume beating (at their own intrinsic rhythm) despite continuous, strong stimulation of the vagus nerve. This occurs because the vagus nerve primarily innervates the SA and AV nodes; if heart rate drops too low, ectopic pacemakers in the Purkinje fibers or ventricles take over to prevent cardiac arrest.

3. Explain the ionic basis for the absolute refractory period observed in the cardiac muscle. [2 marks]

Model Answer

The absolute refractory period (ARP) is primarily due to the inactivation of fast voltage-gated sodium ($Na^+$) channels. During the plateau phase (Phase 2) of the cardiac action potential, these channels remain in an inactive state and cannot be reopened by a second stimulus until the membrane has sufficiently repolarized.

4. What is the physiological significance of a long absolute refractory period in cardiac muscle tissue? [2 marks]

Model Answer

A long ARP ensures that the heart muscle cannot undergo tetanic contractions (summation). This is vital because it allows the ventricles sufficient time to relax and fill with blood between beats, maintaining the pump's efficiency.

5. With respect to the excitation-contraction coupling, give two differences between cardiac and smooth muscles. [2 marks]

Model Answers

1. Calcium Sensor: In cardiac muscle, calcium binds to Troponin C to move tropomyosin. In smooth muscle, calcium binds to Calmodulin, which then activates Myosin Light Chain Kinase (MLCK).

2. Structural Organization: Cardiac muscle is striated with sarcomeres and T-tubules; smooth muscle is non-striated, lacks T-tubules, and utilizes caveolae and dense bodies.

6. Explain briefly the mechanism underlying the therapeutic application of the Na+/K+ ATPase in the management of congestive heart failure. [2 marks]

Model Answer

Cardiac glycosides (like Digoxin) inhibit the Na+/K+ ATPase pump. This leads to an increase in intracellular $Na^+$, which decreases the sodium gradient required for the Sodium-Calcium Exchanger (NCX) to function. Consequently, less $Ca^{2+}$ is extruded from the cell, increasing intracellular $Ca^{2+}$ stores and resulting in increased myocardial contractility (positive inotropy).

8a. What would happen if a player had increased potassium in the ECF? Outline the physiology that would be disrupted at the plasma membrane level of a nerve. [2 marks]

Model Answer

Increased ECF $K^+$ (Hyperkalemia) reduces the concentration gradient for $K^+$ to exit the cell. According to the Nernst equation, this depolarizes the Resting Membrane Potential (RMP), making it more positive (closer to threshold). While initially more excitable, persistent depolarization eventually leads to accommodation where $Na^+$ channels remain in the inactivated state, leading to muscle weakness or cardiac arrhythmias.

8b. Outline the derangements that occur in myasthenia gravis in terms of NMJ in muscle physiology. [5 marks]

Model Answer

Myasthenia Gravis is an autoimmune disorder where antibodies block or destroy Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptors (nAChR) at the post-synaptic motor endplate. This results in:
1. Decreased number of functional receptors.
2. Reduced amplitude of the End-Plate Potential (EPP).
3. Failure to reach threshold to trigger a muscle action potential, leading to progressive muscle weakness and fatigue.

8c. What would happen if a player had hypocalcemia? Outline the physiology of disruption at the level of skeletal muscle membranes. [4 marks]

Model Answer

Hypocalcemia increases membrane excitability. Extracellular $Ca^{2+}$ normally stabilizes voltage-gated $Na^+$ channels by increasing the threshold required for activation. When $Ca^{2+}$ levels are low, the threshold for $Na^+$ channel activation decreases, making the membrane hyper-excitable. This leads to spontaneous repetitive firing of motor neurons, manifesting as hypocalcemic tetany.

9. Compare and contrast the APPLIED significance of osmolarity, osmolality and Tonicity. [5 marks]

Model Answer

Osmolarity is the concentration of osmoles per Liter of solution ($Osm/L$), while Osmolality is per Kilogram of solvent ($Osm/kg$). Clinically, osmolality is preferred because it is independent of temperature/pressure. Tonicity is the most "applied" as it only considers non-penetrating solutes; it determines whether a cell will shrink, swell, or remain stable when placed in a solution (Effective Osmolality).

2. A 35 years old gentleman Mr PP was having pain in his back. He is a veteran NATO soldier of Ukraine- Russia border. 2 years back, he had a surgery of hipbone injury. Now he is complaining of severe back muscle spasms and pain. At local clinic, on examination, his heart rate was 55 per minute, blood electrolytes were disturbed. All other data were normal.

Model Answers

a) Anchoring Proteins: These maintain the structural integrity and alignment of the sarcomere. Titin anchors thick filaments to the Z-discs and acts as a spring, while Nebulin aligns thin filaments. Actinin anchors actin to the Z-discs.
Regulating Proteins: These control the contraction cycle. Tropomyosin blocks the myosin-binding sites on actin, and Troponin (specifically Troponin C) binds $Ca^{2+}$ to shift tropomyosin, allowing cross-bridge formation.

b) IV Fluids by Tonicity:
1. Isotonic: (e.g., 0.9% Normal Saline, Lactated Ringer’s) Used for volume expansion as there is no net fluid shift.
2. Hypotonic: (e.g., 0.45% Saline) Used to treat cellular dehydration by shifting fluid into the cells.
3. Hypertonic: (e.g., 3% Saline, D5NS) Used to shift fluid out of cells into the ECF, often used in severe hyponatremia or cerebral edema.

c) Donnan Effect: Large, non-diffusible intracellular proteins create an electrochemical gradient that draws permeable ions and water into the cell. This "malevolent" role leads to osmotic swelling and potential lysis.
Natural Correction: The Na+/K+ ATPase pump counteracts this by actively extruding 3 $Na^+$ for every 2 $K^+$ it brings in. This net loss of solutes creates an effective osmotic pressure that moves water out, restoring and maintaining normal cell volume.
