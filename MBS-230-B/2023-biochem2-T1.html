1. A 50-year-old male patient was brought to an emergency department of Ndola Teaching Hospital with the diagnosis of myocardial infarction, following determination of different forms of Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) iso-enzymes.

Model Answers

i) They are different molecular forms of the same enzyme that catalyze the same biochemical reaction but differ in their physical/chemical properties.

ii) Yes, many iso-enzymes are predominantly found in specific tissues or organs.

iii) Yes, the pattern or ratio of iso-enzyme distribution varies significantly between different tissues (e.g., LDH1 in heart vs LDH5 in liver).

iv) The elevation of a specific iso-enzyme in plasma points to damage in the specific organ where that iso-enzyme is localized (e.g., LDH1 for myocardial infarction).

v) 5 iso-enzymes: LDH1, LDH2, LDH3, LDH4, and LDH5.

vi) Iso-enzyme activity is organ-specific, whereas total activity may be elevated due to various non-specific reasons. Iso-enzymes provide higher diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.

vii) Electrophoresis (commonly used) or Chromatography.

2. Certain factors have effects on proteins. For example, high temperature denatures most enzymes and it is for this reason that transportation of blood samples would normally be done at reduced temperatures.

Model Answers

i) Denaturation alters the three-dimensional shape of the enzyme (especially the active site), making it unable to bind substrate, which stops or significantly slows the reaction.

ii) It is the specific temperature at which an enzyme exhibits its maximum catalytic activity.

iii) Changes in pH alter the ionization state of amino acids. Extreme pH values cause denaturation; enzymes work best at an optimum pH.

iv) It increases the kinetic energy of the molecules, leading to more frequent and energetic collisions between enzymes and substrates.

v) Primary structure. Denaturants disrupt hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and disulfide bridges (secondary/tertiary), but do not break the covalent peptide bonds of the primary sequence.

vi) Denaturation is a process in which proteins lose their quaternary, tertiary, and secondary structure due to external stress (like heat or pH) while the primary structure remains intact.

vii) 1. Loss of biological activity. 2. Decreased solubility (often leading to coagulation). 3. Increased susceptibility to proteolytic enzymes.

3. Draw a graph of reaction velocity (on the y-axis) of an enzyme catalysed reaction versus substrate concentration (x-axis).

Model Answers

Graph Details: The graph should be a rectangular hyperbola (Michaelis-Menten plot).
Y-axis: Velocity (v)
X-axis: Substrate concentration [S]
Key Labels: Vmax (the horizontal asymptote where velocity plateaus) and Km (the substrate concentration at 1/2 Vmax).

3. (Continued) Enzyme Kinetics

Model Answers

i) Vmax is the horizontal plateau. Vmax/2 is half that height on the y-axis. Km is the substrate concentration (x-axis value) that corresponds to Vmax/2.

ii) Vmax is the maximum velocity of an enzymatic reaction. Km (Michaelis constant) is the substrate concentration at which the reaction velocity is half of its maximum.

iii) 1. Beginning: Velocity increases linearly with substrate concentration (First-order kinetics). 2. Transition: The rate of increase slows down. 3. Vmax/Plateau: The enzyme is saturated; further increases in substrate do not increase velocity (Zero-order kinetics).

iv) When Vmax is reached, the reaction velocity levels off because all enzyme active sites are occupied (saturated) by substrate molecules. There is no free enzyme remaining to process additional substrate.

4. Proteins do everything in living cells. They are responsible for many different functions in a cell... Most of the amino acids in proteins are optically active.

Model Answers

i) Simple proteins: Yield only amino acids on hydrolysis. Conjugated proteins: Contain a non-protein group (prosthetic group). Derived proteins: Produced by the action of heat or chemical reagents (denaturation/hydrolysis).

ii) Fibrous proteins: Long, thread-like molecules (e.g., collagen, keratin). Globular proteins: Spherical or ellipsoidal shapes (e.g., hemoglobin, enzymes).

iii) Primary: Linear sequence of amino acids. Secondary: Local folding like alpha-helices and beta-sheets. Tertiary: Overall 3D folding of a single chain. Quaternary: Arrangement of multiple polypeptide subunits.

iv) L-Alanine (or any L-amino acid except Glycine).

v) 1. They rotate plane-polarized light. 2. They exist as non-superimposable mirror images (enantiomers).

5. A mutation in a gene that codes for the beta chain in hemoglobin causes sickle cell disease...

Model Answers

i) Glutamic acid is substituted by Valine at the 6th position of the beta chain.

ii) Sickle-shaped red blood cells are rigid and block small capillaries (vaso-occlusion). They also have a lower surface area for gas exchange and a shorter lifespan (hemolysis), leading to anemia and reduced oxygen delivery.

iii) Because the mutation results in the production of hemoglobin with an altered structure (abnormal quality), rather than a reduced amount of normal hemoglobin (which would be quantitative, like Thalassemia).

iv) Methemoglobin.

v) No.

vi) To function as a carrier capable of binding and releasing oxygen reversibly. Only the reduced state (Fe2+) can bind oxygen.

7. A polypeptide chain has to attain a native conformation as soon as it comes out of the protein synthesizing machinery...

Model Answers

i) 1. Formation of local secondary structures. 2. Formation of domains. 3. Final molten globule state. 4. Final tertiary structure adjustment.

ii) The primary structure (amino acid sequence).

iii) Protein Disulfide Isomerase (PDI) and Peptidyl Prolyl Isomerase (PPI).

iv) Chaperones (or Chaperonins).

v) They bind to exposed hydrophobic regions of unfolded or partially folded polypeptide chains, shielding them from improper interactions and preventing them from sticking together (aggregating) until the correct folding can occur.

8. Sulfonamide antibiotics are used in the treatment of bacterial infections. Bacteria synthesizes folic acid from p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA). Sulfonamides contain sulfanilamide, a structural analog of PABA. When used as chemotherapeutic agents, sulfonamides block the synthesis of folic acid in bacteria.

Model Answers

i) Competitive inhibition (because the drug is a structural analog of the substrate).

ii) No, Vmax remains unchanged. Competitive inhibition can be overcome by increasing the substrate concentration.

iii) The Km is increased (apparent Km increases), meaning more substrate is needed to reach half of the maximum velocity.

iv) Km is inversely related to affinity. A higher Km value indicates a lower affinity of the enzyme for its substrate, while a lower Km indicates a higher affinity.

v) Examples include Cyanide (inhibits cytochrome oxidase), Diisopropyl fluorophosphate (DFP/nerve gas), or Penicillin (inhibits bacterial transpeptidase).
